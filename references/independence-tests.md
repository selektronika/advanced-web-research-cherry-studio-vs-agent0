# Independence Tests

Independence comes from the **origin and method** of the evidence, not from the number of
providers queried or pages that repeat it. Three search engines returning the same ten URLs
is one origin.

## Ask of each pair of agreeing sources

- Do they rely on the same first-party assertion, dataset, press release, wire copy, or
  interview?
- Are they the same publisher, the same institutional family, or a licensee of the same
  content?
- Did one cite the other, or a shared upstream source?
- Do they use different methods, samples, data collection, or measurement periods?
- Is either an interested party with an incentive toward the claim?
- Do they differ in definition, scope, or population enough that agreement is coincidental
  rather than corroborating?

If any answer reveals a shared origin, treat them as **one origin**. Say so in the answer:
"three outlets report this figure; all trace to the operator's statement."

## Tracing repeated reporting

1. Find the earliest dated instance of the claim.
2. Identify the original source it rests on.
3. Note whether later coverage adds new evidence, a new method, or only a restatement.
4. Report the claim with its **true evidential weight**, not the appearance created by
   repetition.

## Red flags

- Identical or near-identical phrasing across outlets — syndication.
- One anonymous source behind many articles — a single origin wearing many names.
- Statistics without a retrievable dataset, methodology, or audit trail.
- A claim that cites itself through circular references.
- Strong conclusions drawn from methodologically weak or interested sources.
- A number that appears "everywhere" but originates in one press release or one vendor
  estimate.
- Dates, vintages, or definitions that drift between repeats.
- Translation chains that quietly alter a term of record.
- Search-result pages, AI summaries, and LLM-context chunks presented as the evidence.

## Positive indicators of genuine independence

- Different institutional roles (regulator, auditor, competitor, academic, journalist).
- Different methods (administrative register, sensor measurement, survey, direct
  observation, computation from raw data).
- Different incentive positions (a competitor confirming a rival's weakness is meaningful
  corroboration; a supplier confirming its own quality is not).
- Independent replication or a null result that the original claim would have to explain
  away.

## Practical rule for the ledger

Record, per material claim, how many genuinely independent origins support it and name the
weakest link. Two independent origins with a disclosed single-origin caveat beats ten
derivative pages; one strong primary record can settle a D0 question outright.
